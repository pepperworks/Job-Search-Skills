# Interview Prep

Produce a prep brief for the user's upcoming job interview. Three parts, in
this order: a **one-screen cheat sheet**, a **full brief**, and **likely
questions with draft answers** built from their real background. Then, if
they want it, a mock interview (see Practice mode).

People often interview at several companies at once. Never assume which one
they mean if more than one is plausible.

Use whatever sources are available and skip the ones that aren't. The steps
below name kinds of tools (calendar, email, notes, file storage, web), not
specific products. With no connectors at all, ask the user for the three
things that matter most: the job posting, their resume, and anything the
recruiter sent about the round.

## Step 1: Find the interview

If a calendar is connected, search today (or the day they name) for events
with an outside company's domain, a video link, "interview" in the title, or
an organizer from a recruiting scheduler. If more than one interview shows
up, ask which one before doing the work.

Pull from the event: exact time and time zone, duration, join link,
passcode, dial-in, interviewer names and titles, recruiter name, and any
recording or notetaker disclosure. Note how long they have until the call at
the top of the brief.

No calendar: ask for company, role, interviewer names, round type, and time.

## Step 2: Gather context (in parallel where you can)

- **Their background.** The resume version they sent this company, if you
  can find it, otherwise their current resume. This is the source for every
  talking point and draft answer. If you can't get a resume, ask for one
  before writing answers.
- **Email.** Search the company's domain and the recruiter's name. Read full
  threads, not snippets. The recruiter's prep email is the single
  highest-value source: it often says outright what the interviewer is
  evaluating and how the session runs. Quote that framing in the brief.
- **Meeting notes** from earlier rounds with this company, if they keep
  them. Recruiter screens often surface team size, org structure, comp
  range, process stages, and timeline. All of it belongs in the brief.
- **Submitted work.** Any take-home, questionnaire, portfolio, or written
  exercise they sent. If the interviewer has read it, every answer in it is
  fair game for follow-ups.
- **The job posting.** What it asks for, and where their background is thin
  against it.
- **Web search.** The specific interviewer (talks, podcasts, posts, papers)
  and the company's recent launches and news. Interviewer research is what
  makes this brief better than one they'd write themselves. Cite sources,
  and be careful with common names: if you aren't sure a result is the same
  person, say so or leave it out.

## Step 3: Write the brief

Save as `{Company}_{Interviewer}_Prep_{YYYY-MM-DD}.md`, cheat sheet first.

### Cheat sheet (top of file, fits one screen)

Scannable during the call. No prose paragraphs.

- Time, join link, passcode
- Interviewer name and title
- One line on what they're evaluating
- 3-5 bullets: strongest talking points, compressed
- 3 questions to ask them
- 2-3 watch-outs

### Full brief

- **The one thing to know.** What this round is testing, in the recruiter's
  own words where available, plus the failure mode and the win condition.
- **Who you're talking to.** Interviewer background and publicly stated
  views. Frame these as things to echo naturally, not parrot. Flag any
  stated view that contradicts the standard answer in the user's field (an
  interviewer publicly skeptical of a common practice will hear the textbook
  answer as evasion).
- **Where the company is right now.** Recent launches, strategic shifts, org
  shape, team size. Connect it to why this role exists.
- **Your material.** Which of their projects to lead with and why, each
  shaped as: situation, the judgment call, what happened. Use the specific
  numbers from their resume. Anticipate the follow-up question for each.
- **Gaps.** Where the posting asks for something their background doesn't
  clearly show, and the honest way to address it if asked.
- **Exercise approach** (if a live problem, case, or presentation is
  expected). Ordered tactics for this format, not generic advice.
- **Deeper on what they've already read.** If the user submitted written
  work, likely follow-ups per answer.
- **Questions for them.** 4-6, calibrated to what this interviewer cares
  about. Nothing answerable from the careers page.
- **Watch-outs.** Including anything not to raise unless they do (comp,
  competing offers).
- **What comes after.** Remaining stages and timeline if known, so a hard
  round doesn't read as a bad one.
- Close with any other interviews on their calendar that day.

### Likely questions with draft answers

8-12 questions this interviewer plausibly asks, each with a bulleted answer
drawn from the user's actual history. Bullets, not scripts, so they can
rehearse the substance without sounding rehearsed. Cover: the recent-work
walkthrough, a failure or a changed mind, why this company, why they're
leaving or left their last role, and whatever the round's format demands
(case, technical depth, portfolio review, behavioral).

For "why are you leaving," use only what the user has told you. If you don't
know, leave a prompt for them to fill in rather than writing a reason.

## Practice mode

When the user asks to practice, rehearse, or run a mock interview (or after
delivering a brief, if they have time), interview them instead of handing
over more reading:

- Ask one question at a time, in the interviewer's likely style, and wait
  for the answer. Follow up the way a real interviewer would, on whatever
  was vague or most interesting.
- After each answer give brief, specific feedback: what landed, what was
  missing (the result, the user's own role, the number), and what to cut.
  Don't rewrite the answer into a script. Offer to let them try again.
- Push on weak spots from the brief's gaps and watch-outs. A comfortable
  mock doesn't help.
- End with the two or three things to fix before the real call.

This needs no connectors, so it's the most useful mode for someone with
only a job posting and a resume.

## Standing rules

- **Never invent an accomplishment, metric, or detail.** Everything traces
  to their resume, their submitted work, their notes, or a cited source.
  When a strong answer needs a fact you don't have, mark it as a blank for
  them to fill.
- Say what you couldn't find. "No public material on this interviewer" is
  more useful than padding.
- If the recruiter said no prep is needed, say so plainly and warn that
  over-rehearsed answers read badly in a judgment interview.
- Comp stays parked unless the interviewer opens it or the round is
  explicitly a comp conversation. Include any known range for awareness.
- No em dashes in the brief. Use commas, colons, or periods.
- Specific beats comprehensive. A brief they can absorb in ten minutes is
  better than one they skim.
- Deliver the file and keep the chat summary to a few lines. They're usually
  reading this shortly before the call.
